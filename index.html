Professional Self-Assessment

Completing the Computer Science program has changed the way I approach technology, problem-solving, and software development. When I began the program, much of my focus was on learning how to make programs work. As I progressed through more advanced courses, I learned that professional software development involves much more than producing working code. It requires understanding requirements, making design decisions, considering security, communicating technical information, testing solutions, working with different technologies, and creating software that other people can understand and maintain. Developing this ePortfolio has given me the opportunity to look back at that growth and demonstrate how my skills have developed across several areas of computer science.

One of my strengths is my ability to work with different technologies and adapt to unfamiliar problems. Throughout the program, I worked with languages and technologies including C++, Java, Python, JavaScript, TypeScript, SQL, MongoDB, Angular, Node.js, Android development tools, and Raspberry Pi hardware. Courses involving graphics, artificial intelligence, mobile development, embedded systems, databases, full-stack development, secure coding, and reverse engineering required different ways of thinking. These experiences helped me become more comfortable learning new tools rather than relying on a single programming language or development environment.

My experience has also taught me the importance of collaboration and communication in computer science. During my software engineering internship, I worked on a JavaScript web application while participating in regular Microsoft Teams meetings and completing both front-end and back-end tasks. That experience showed me how different professional development can be from working independently on a school assignment. Developers need to communicate their progress, understand the work being completed by other team members, respond to feedback, and make sure their contributions fit into the larger project.

Communicating with stakeholders is another area in which I have developed throughout the program. Software is ultimately created to solve a problem for someone, whether that person is a customer, user, manager, or another developer. Several projects required me to move from written requirements to working software while considering how users would interact with the finished system. These experiences helped me understand that successful developers must be able to translate between technical requirements and the needs of the people who depend on the software.

Data structures and algorithms have strengthened my approach to solving problems efficiently. Earlier in the program, I often focused primarily on whether a program produced the correct result. As my coursework progressed, I became more aware that there can be several correct solutions to the same problem, but some solutions are more efficient, maintainable, or scalable than others. Working with sorting, searching, collections, and other data structures taught me to evaluate how information is stored and processed instead of automatically choosing the first solution that works.

Software engineering and database development have been two of the most important areas of my growth. Through full-stack development, I learned how the different layers of an application work together. I gained experience connecting a user interface to application logic, REST APIs, authentication systems, and persistent database storage. My database coursework taught me that storing information is only one part of database development. Data must also be structured logically, retrieved efficiently, validated, protected, and presented in a useful way to the rest of an application.

Security has also become a much larger part of the way I think about software. My secure coding and reverse engineering coursework showed me that functionality alone does not determine whether software is well designed. Developers also have to consider how software could fail or be intentionally misused. I worked with issues such as buffer overflows, SQL injection, numeric errors, exception handling, authentication weaknesses, and input validation. These experiences taught me to think about security throughout development instead of treating it as something that should only be checked after a program is finished.

Building my ePortfolio has given me another opportunity to apply these lessons. Instead of simply presenting completed assignments, I returned to previous work and examined how it could be improved. Reviewing older projects showed me areas where my programming practices, organization, documentation, security, and design decisions have developed since the projects were originally created.

The artifacts in this portfolio work together to demonstrate different parts of my development as a computer science professional. The software design and engineering enhancement demonstrates my ability to examine an existing application and improve its design and functionality. The algorithms and data structures enhancement demonstrates my ability to apply sorting, searching, and other algorithmic concepts while considering the effectiveness of the solution. The database enhancement demonstrates my ability to work with persistent data as part of a larger application and improve the way software interacts with stored information. The code review connects these areas by showing the planning and analysis that took place before the enhancements were completed.

Together, these artifacts represent more than three individual projects. They demonstrate my ability to analyze existing software, identify opportunities for improvement, implement technical solutions, communicate my decisions, and evaluate the results. They also show that my education has prepared me to continue learning as technologies and professional expectations change.

As I complete the Computer Science program, my goal is to begin a career where I can continue developing my skills while contributing to real software and technology systems. I understand that earning a degree does not mean that the learning process is finished. Computer science is constantly changing, and becoming a successful professional requires continued learning, adaptability, communication, and responsibility. The combination of my coursework, previous technical experience, and the work represented in this ePortfolio has given me a strong foundation for that next step.
